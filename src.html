WHERE IT
BREAKS

Five failures, and four of them are structural.

None of these is a tooling gap that a better spreadsheet closes. Each is a seam between two teams who hold different units, or a quantity treated as a constant that is not one.

  • Stages 3 and 4 do not share a unit. Sizing produces accelerators. Siting needs kilowatts, square metres and a date. That translation is done once, informally, by whoever is in the room — and every later number inherits it.
  • Announced capacity enters the model as supply. A campus, a megawatt figure and a year become a planning input. It is an intention gated by an interconnection queue the provider does not control, and the queues are getting longer.
  • Agentic workloads are sized with chat arithmetic. One user action is treated as one inference when it is a recursive tool-invocation series with a stochastic depth. The error is roughly an order of magnitude, in the direction that leaves you short.
  • Hard constraints are scored rather than excluded. Put residency and cost in the same weighted model and a sufficiently cheap non-compliant site wins. Being wrong about export control is a criminal matter, not a false-positive rate.
  • Stage 9 has no owner. The supply graph moves monthly; the decision is annual at best. Nothing re-runs it, so the gap between what was decided and what is now true widens silently for the whole life of the asset.

VYUHA is a solver with a narrative layer. The kernel computes; the language model composes and is structurally prevented from originating a quantity — enforced by a binding registry and a numeral scanner, not by asking a model nicely in a prompt.

Physics before language

Every number on an output traces to the kernel or to a dated measurement. The narrative layer may only compose what the solver produced.

Every answer is dated

A coefficient without a measurement date is unusable rather than defaulted. The refusal to size on an unmeasured coefficient is the architecture, not a gap in it.

Negative results are outputs

WHY THE ANSWERS HOLD

Four rules that are tested, not asserted.

01

Exclusions, never penalties

Hard constraints cannot be bought off.

An ITAR residency breach is not offset by a cost saving, because there is no score anywhere that could trade them. Precedence is LEGALITY > PHYSICS > DELIVERABILITY > ECONOMICS > PREFERENCE, arbitrated and never summed.

02

Unconfirmed is a value

A site fact nobody confirmed is not a fact with a default.

GPU generation, chassis density and fabric parse as UNCONFIRMED. Plan works without them and names every gap with its remedy; apply refuses. There is no force flag, because a default provisions the wrong lab and nobody finds out until week nine.

03

Idempotence is tested

The second pass must be empty.

The reconciler returns every pass and the suite asserts the second is empty. That assertion found a real defect: a priority class emitted once per tenant produced duplicate resource ids, which would have rewritten the object on every pass for ever.

04

Escalations resolve

An unresolvable gate is how teams learn to bypass gates.

Where a question is a determination for an empowered official rather than a computation, the engine reports UNEVALUABLE and clears it when the blueprint records who determined it, when, and in what scope — not via a flag that switches the check off.

Why a repository, not a cluster

A reconciler that also wrote to the cluster would be a second way for the lab to change, one that leaves no commit. The git log would stop being the complete record of intent, which is the whole reason the audit trail is worth anything.

The policy engine has a caller

An admission webhook that fails closed under strict mode, reads personnel status from the API server's own user info rather than from a pod annotation a tenant could write, and records every decision in the chained ledger before returning it.

The portal competes with its own promises

Outstanding grants are subtracted from available capacity, because observed utilisation lags every grant by however long the holder takes to start work. That is how a self-service platform approves six requests in a morning that all collide in the afternoon.

No SKU is hardcoded

Every hardware fact comes from a generation table keyed by the string the blueprint supplied, with per-entry provenance. Lookup is exact or case-insensitive, never fuzzy. A MIG request on a generation whose geometry is not recorded reports untested rather than being validated against a neighbour's profiles.

STAGE 7
THE EGRESS CALL

It does not read the prompt. It cannot, and neither can anything else.

The admission webhook approved the pod days ago. Procurement approved the endpoint last quarter. Between them sits this request, from this engineer, carrying this payload — and nothing in the stack decides it.

VYUHA decides it on three things: the workload's declared classification, the principal's asserted attributes, and the destination's recorded assessment. The API has no field for prompt text at all.

Every competitor ships a classifier that reads the payload and judges it. A classifier saying "this looks unclassified" is a probabilistic opinion standing exactly where a legal determination belongs, and being wrong about export control is a criminal matter rather than a false-positive rate.

An undeclared workload is denied here. It is not scanned, not inferred, and not waved through on a confidence score.

EVIDENCE

Git records what was declared. The ledger records what was attempted.

The chain breaks where it is touched

The hash-chained ledger records what was attempted, by whom, on whose authority, against what evidence. A deleted entry breaks the chain at the following entry; an edited one no longer hashes to its digest.

Tamper-evident rather than tamper-proof, and the distinction is the point: an auditor who does not trust the operator can still check it. That is a weaker claim than most vendors make and a more useful one.

Every solve records the supply-graph snapshot it ran against, so a figure quoted in a board paper in March can be reproduced bit-identically in November and compared with what the same solve returns today.

Attribution says what it cannot attribute

Exclusive and partitioned consumption is exact. Time-sliced consumption shared between tenants carries a band bounded by the two extremes the telemetry cannot rule out, and the band is printed on the statement rather than resolved to a single number for tidiness.

Unlabelled busy time stays in the residual instead of being spread across tenants to make the rows sum to a hundred. A statement that always reconciles exactly is a statement that has been made to.

Where a figure rests on an unmeasured coefficient the arithmetic still runs and the figures are still shown — labelled unproven, with every finding derived from them withdrawn, except where the finding holds at the best coefficient physics allows. A shortfall the memory floor forces is geometry rather than a guess.

WORKFLOW · NOVEMBER 2026 · 6 MIN

The stage with no owner

Every other stage of the AI factory workflow has a name on it. The one that would catch the decision going wrong does not.

Write out the workflow that takes an enterprise from a board commitment to tokens being served and you get nine stages. Mandate. Demand. Sizing. Siting. Structure. Build. Admit. Account. And then the ninth, which is simply: is this still the right decision.

Eight of those have an owner. You can name the person, find their calendar, see their artifact. The ninth has none. Not a weak owner, not a contested one — none. It is on no org chart I have been shown, in no RACI, in no operating rhythm.

Why it went missing

Nothing conspired to remove it. It was never created, because the workflow was inherited from a world where the answer held.

Enterprise infrastructure decisions used to be stable for their asset life. You sized a VMware estate, you bought it, and the assumptions underneath it moved slowly enough that a three-year refresh cycle was adequate governance. Nobody needed a standing stage to ask whether the decision had gone stale, because it mostly had not.

AI infrastructure does not behave that way. The accelerator generation turns over faster than the procurement cycle. Interconnection queues lengthen. Jurisdictional rules on where models and data may sit are being written while the capital is being spent. And the workload itself changes shape underneath you, most sharply when a chat deployment becomes an agentic one and the inference count per user action moves by an order of magnitude.

So the decision now decays, and the workflow that produced it has no step that notices.

What the absence costs

The cost is invisible by construction, which is why it goes unmanaged. There is no incident, no outage, no failed audit. There is only a widening gap between what was assumed in a spreadsheet in March and what is true in the market in November, and the gap is funded for five years.

Occasionally it surfaces. A programme discovers that the site it committed to cannot be energized until two quarters after it needed to be live. An estate sized for chat traffic falls over the first week the agents go into production. A jurisdictional rule turns a cost-optimal placement into a compliance finding. Each gets treated as a surprise and a post-mortem. None gets treated as what it is: the predictable output of a workflow with no re-adjudication stage.

Why nobody has simply added it

Because at the moment, re-adjudicating means re-running stages two through five by hand. That is the same four scarce people and the same six to fourteen weeks that produced the original answer. No organisation is going to spend a quarter of specialist time per quarter to check an answer, so the honest institutional response is to not check it.

That is the actual blocker. It is not that nobody wants to know; it is that knowing is priced at the cost of the original decision, every time.

Which means the ninth stage cannot be created by assigning it to someone. It only becomes possible once the four stages underneath it are a solve against a dated snapshot rather than a bespoke piece of consulting — because then re-adjudication is running the same solve against a newer snapshot and reading the diff, and the question stops costing a quarter to ask.

PLACEMENT · NOVEMBER 2026 · 6 MIN

Announced capacity is not rentable capacity

The cheapest placement in the comparison is frequently the one that cannot be energized until after the programme needed it.

Every placement comparison I have been shown in the last year leads with price per GPU-hour. It is the column everyone has, which is the only real reason it is first. It is also the column least likely to decide whether the programme succeeds, because it answers a question that only matters once a prior question has been answered affirmatively: can this capacity actually be serving by the date we need it.

The industry has learned to treat announced capacity as a planning input. A provider announces a campus, a megawatt figure and a year, and that figure enters somebody's model as supply. It is not supply. It is an intention, gated by an interconnection queue that the provider does not control, in a process whose timelines have been getting longer rather than shorter.

What the gate actually checks

The useful test is narrow and unglamorous. For each candidate placement, is the power block's grid-interconnection state confirmed by the date the programme requires, and if it is not, the candidate is excluded. Not penalised, not flagged amber, not carried forward with a caveat in a footnote. Excluded, because a placement that cannot be energized in time is not a worse option than the others, it is not an option at all.

This is the same discipline as treating hard constraints as exclusions rather than penalties, and it fails in the same way when you relax it. The moment energization becomes a weighted factor, a sufficiently attractive price can outvote it, and the model will cheerfully recommend a site that will be ready eight months after the business needed to be live.

Why answers come back in megawatts and months

Once energization is a gate rather than a factor, the shape of the answer changes. The output is no longer a ranked list of dollar figures. It is a smaller set of placements that close at all, each carrying the power envelope it needs and the date it can meet, and the dollar comparison happens inside that set.

Executives find this uncomfortable at first because it removes options. That is the point. The options it removes were never available; they were artefacts of a model that treated an announcement as a commitment.

The second-order effect

There is a consequence that only shows up once you have run the gate across many programmes. Infeasibility stops being an error and becomes data. Aggregate the placements that could not close, and across a corpus you have a measure of demand that the market cannot currently serve, broken down by the constraint that blocked it — jurisdiction, power, delivery date, or silicon that does not exist yet in the quantity required.

Nobody publishes that number because nobody is structured to collect it. A tool that treats infeasibility as an exception throws the information away at the moment it is generated. Treating it as a schema instead costs nothing and compounds.

SIZING · NOVEMBER 2026 · 5 MIN

Your agentic estate is not a chat estate with more users

One user action is a recursive tool-invocation series with a stochastic depth, and capacity plans carry a single number for it.

Ask an infrastructure team how they sized their 2027 agentic estate and you will usually get a defensible-sounding chain: expected users, actions per user per day, tokens per action, tokens per second per accelerator, accelerators. Each step is arithmetic nobody can argue with. The answer is still wrong, often by close to an order of magnitude, and it is wrong at the second step.

In a chat system, a user action is one inference. Prompt in, completion out. The mapping is one to one and the arithmetic above is sound.

In an agentic system it is nothing of the sort. A single user action triggers a planning turn, then a tool call, then the tool result re-entering context, then another planning turn conditioned on that result, possibly a retry when the tool fails, possibly a sub-agent with its own series, and a summarisation pass at the end. The user sees one question and one answer. The accelerators saw somewhere between four and forty inferences, each with its own prefill against a context that grew at every step.

The distribution is the finding

The instinct at this point is to find the multiplier — to decide that agentic actions cost, say, twelve times a chat action, and carry on with the original arithmetic. That is better than nothing and it still throws away the thing that matters, which is that the fan-out is a distribution rather than a constant.

Depth varies with the query. A simple lookup resolves in two turns; an ambiguous request that requires three tools and a retry resolves in twenty. Capacity is not sized against the mean of that distribution, because the estate has to stay up during the hours when the hard queries cluster. It is sized against a tail, and you cannot reason about a tail you have replaced with an average.

Worse, the prefill cost grows with depth. Each tool result re-enters context, so the fifth inference in a series is processing a materially longer prompt than the first. The series does not cost depth multiplied by the first call; it costs more than that, and the relationship is not linear.

Why it fails in the direction it does

Sizing errors are not symmetric in their consequences. An estate sized too large wastes capital, which is visible, embarrassing and survivable. An estate sized too small fails during exactly the periods when the hard queries arrive, which is when the system is most load-bearing and most watched.

Chat-era arithmetic fails in the second direction, every time, because every simplification it makes — one inference per action, mean rather than tail, constant prefill — moves the answer the same way.

What to do instead

Size the series, not the call. Characterise the fan-out distribution for the workload class rather than assuming a multiplier, carry the growing context cost explicitly, and plan against a tail percentile that you have chosen deliberately rather than inherited by accident.

None of that is difficult arithmetic. It is just arithmetic nobody does, because the first model anyone built was a chat model and the spreadsheet was never revisited when the workload changed underneath it.

ADMISSIBILITY · NOVEMBER 2026 · 5 MIN

The third value

Every admissibility check has three outcomes. Most tools carry two, and what they do with the third decides whether anyone still uses the gate a year later.

A constraint check can come back three ways. It passed. It failed. Or it could not be evaluated — the fact it depends on was never confirmed, the geometry was never recorded, or the question is a legal determination rather than a computation and no amount of compute resolves it.

Almost every tool in this space carries a boolean. Which means that at the moment the third case arises, something has to decide which of the two buckets it falls into, and both choices are wrong in ways that take months to surface.

Calling it a pass

Map unevaluable onto pass and the system becomes confidently incorrect. The report is green. The constraint was never tested. Nobody who reads the output can distinguish a check that ran and succeeded from a check that could not run at all, because by the time it reaches them the distinction has been erased.

This is the failure mode that produces the worst outcomes, because it is invisible precisely in proportion to how much the organisation trusts the tool. The more authoritative the green, the further the unevaluated risk travels.

Calling it a failure

Map it onto failure instead and the system becomes correct and unusable, which is a slower route to the same place. Teams encounter gates that block on questions the engine cannot answer and that no amount of engineering effort will clear, because the blocker is a determination awaiting a human with authority.

What happens next is predictable. Somebody adds a flag to skip the check. The flag is used for the legitimate case, then for the borderline case, then by default. Within two quarters the gate is nominally in force and functionally disabled, and nobody decided to disable it. An unresolvable gate is how teams learn to bypass gates.

Carrying it

The alternative is to make unevaluable a first-class verdict that travels all the way to the output, and to give it a route to resolution that is not a bypass.

In practice that means three things. The verdict appears on the report as itself, so a reader can see that nine checks passed and two could not be assessed. The engine names the specific missing fact and its remedy rather than reporting a general failure. And the resolution path records who made the determination, when, and in what scope — so the check clears because a question was answered by somebody accountable, not because a flag switched it off.

That last distinction is the whole thing. A gate cleared by an authority creates a record. A gate cleared by a flag creates nothing, and the next person to read the output cannot tell which happened.
